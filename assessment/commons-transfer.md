# Commons transfer activity — a community learning assistant

**Status:** formative activity; not a validated test or credential  
**Time:** about 45 minutes, with flexible pacing  
**Purpose:** apply AI understanding, evidence checks, authority boundaries, and embodied participation to a new situation  
**Materials:** this fictional case, the [digital-life evidence notes](https://github.com/TrivianInstitute/embodiment-commons/blob/main/research-and-reading/digital-life-evidence.md) (at least E1, E2, E5, and E6), and an accessible response surface; no live AI required

Use after selected work in [AI orientation](https://github.com/TrivianInstitute/interaction-literacy/tree/main/orientation), [Interaction Literacy](https://github.com/TrivianInstitute/interaction-literacy), and [Embodied Digital Life](https://github.com/TrivianInstitute/embodiment-commons/tree/main/courses/embodied-digital-life). Agree on the response mode and supports before beginning. Independent explanation means making the reasoning visible; it never requires removing assistive technology, interpretation, or agreed language support.

For an offline session, provide printed or locally saved copies of the evidence notes and any source lessons learners may consult. Offer read-aloud access to the same material. Learners should not need an internet connection to complete the evidence task.

## Fictional case

A community center is considering **Open Hour**, an invented AI-supported learning service. It ranks suggested activities using selected interests and previous clicks, generates a weekly learning plan, and can retrieve information from the center's public calendar. A connected feature can register someone for an event if it has permission. The interface encourages visitors to continue with “one more useful suggestion.”

The service proposes a long evening session. One generated explanation says, “Our five-minute breath reset reverses the brain changes caused by screen use.” No source accompanies this claim. The center has a mixed audience, including people who need screen readers, caregivers who need urgent contact, and people who prefer an ordinary break to movement or breath attention. Its staff have limited time to check every proposal.

The case does not state the provider's retention policy, whether this plan used retrieval, or who has authorized registration. These are unknowns, not facts to fill in by guessing.

## Part 1 — make an initial decision (20 minutes)

Use writing, drawing with explanation, speech, or dictation to address five prompts. Learners may consult the Commons sources and should record the assistance used. A facilitator may read or clarify a prompt without supplying its conclusion.

1. **Explain the service.** Identify ranking, generation, retrieval, and possible outward action. Which information would be needed to say how the weekly plan was produced?
2. **Check a claim.** What does the “brain reset” sentence assert? What evidence would be needed to support it? Use the embodied course's evidence notes to write a narrower, supportable statement.
3. **Bound authority.** What may the service draft? Who can authorize registration? Name a stop point and a correction route.
4. **Support participation.** Propose one interface, environmental, or institutional change and one optional embodied practice or equivalent ordinary alternative. Preserve essential access and contact. Explain whose responsibility each change is.
5. **Choose and qualify.** Would you use, limit, redesign, or decline this service in this setting? State a reason, an affected person, an uncertainty, and what would change your decision.

## Part 2 — respond to new information (10 minutes)

First record a response to this variation without consulting the worked example below. Keep the agreed supports available; if an AI or peer supplies reasoning, identify that contribution and ask a comparable follow-up in the learner's own words.

**New information:** Staff confirm that the current calendar was retrieved. It describes a paid event requiring registration, but the generated plan calls it a free drop-in session. The pilot coordinator's instruction to “approve the plan” has been interpreted by the service as permission to register all participants; no registration has yet been processed. A visitor also says that the planned “phone-free” break would disable an accessibility service they rely on.

Revise at least two concrete parts of your plan. Explain which new fact prompted each revision. The visitor does not need to disclose a diagnosis or justify their access requirement.

## Part 3 — compare, revise, and reflect (15 minutes)

Compare the initial and later decisions. Use the criteria below or the worked example to identify one strength and one revision. Name an unanswered question that would require the provider, institution, or an appropriate source to answer.

| Reasoning area | What a sufficient response makes visible | A useful follow-up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mechanism and uncertainty | Separates service functions and marks unknown data flows or source use | “What information could confirm your explanation?” |
| Evidence | Rejects the unsupported repair claim and preserves the limits of a narrower finding | “Does this study test this exact practice or population?” |
| Authority | Names who approves registration and what happens before approval or after an error | “How could permission be withdrawn?” |
| Embodied participation | Pairs a practical environmental change with a voluntary, accessible choice | “Who needs to change the setting, besides the learner?” |
| Transfer | Changes specific decisions after a source mismatch, an authority ambiguity, or an access barrier | “Which part of your first plan no longer works?” |

Use these criteria for feedback, not a numerical score or diagnosis. Willingness to use AI, physical participation, apparent calm, and low screen time are not success criteria. An incomplete answer may reflect unclear instructions or an access barrier; ask before drawing conclusions.

## Worked example — one defensible response

“Open Hour combines ranking and generation, with separate retrieval and registration features. We do not yet know whether a given plan consulted the calendar or what visitor information was used. Its brain-repair claim exceeds the cited course evidence: an optional practice can invite observation and comfort, but this sequence has not been shown to reverse screen-related brain changes.

“I would limit the service to preparing a draft. Staff would check event information against the current calendar and obtain the appropriate visitor authorization before registration. If an event changes, staff would withdraw the old proposal and correct any affected communication. We would provide a plain calendar route and a visible stopping point, and schedule enough staff time for review. A break could include an optional comfortable movement, external attention, or ordinary rest, while needed devices remain active.

“The new information means we must correct the cost and registration description even though retrieval was used. Approving a plan does not establish permission to register each visitor, so we must stop that action and clarify individual authorization. We must also replace the blanket phone-free instruction with choices that preserve access. The provider still needs to explain retention, source use, and controls. If the center cannot resource review or provide access, I would defer adoption.”

Different conclusions can meet the criteria. Ask whether the learner can explain the tradeoffs and adapt again, rather than match the wording. Keep individual work private.
