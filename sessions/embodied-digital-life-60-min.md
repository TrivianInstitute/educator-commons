# Embodied digital life — a 60-minute introduction

**Status:** formative session plan; not piloted or clinically validated  
**Audience:** adults and older secondary learners, adapted to the setting  
**Aim:** distinguish a digital-life observation from an explanation, then choose an environmental change and an optional practice  
**Source:** [Embodied Digital Life: Attention, Breath, Movement, and Rest](https://github.com/TrivianInstitute/embodiment-commons/tree/main/courses/embodied-digital-life) and its [evidence notes](https://github.com/TrivianInstitute/embodiment-commons/blob/main/research-and-reading/digital-life-evidence.md)  
**Materials:** this page and the selected practice text, with a written or read-aloud option; no live AI or personal tracking data required  
**Output:** a bounded explanation and a revisable digital-life plan

This is a teaching session about attention, digital conditions, and choice. It introduces selected course material; completing it is not completion of the six-unit course or a qualification to provide yoga, clinical care, or assisted practice.

## Prepare the choices

Read the course's scope and [facilitation guide](https://github.com/TrivianInstitute/embodiment-commons/blob/main/teaching-guides/facilitating.md). Make ordinary rest, external attention, and fictional-case analysis available before inviting any movement or breath attention. Do not require closed eyes, standing, touch, a particular posture, or a breathing pace. Keep assistive devices, urgent contact channels, and other needed supports available.

Introduce the options in equal terms: “You can try an optional practice, notice an ordinary feature of the room, take a regular break, or continue working with the fictional case. You can change your mind. There is no expected feeling and nothing personal needs to be shared.”

### Fictional situation: a difficult transition

**Noor is an invented adult learner.** Noor uses a phone for study, connection, and access support. During a long AI-assisted planning session, notifications repeatedly appear. Afterward, Noor continues scrolling because the next item seems interesting. Noor notices tense shoulders and a later bedtime on that particular evening. A colleague says, “The dopamine hits have damaged your brain; a breathing exercise will reset it.”

These are details of a fictional case, not a diagnosis or evidence that one event caused another. Ask the group to preserve the useful functions of Noor's technology while considering what could change.

## Run the session

| Time | Activity | Facilitation focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–5 | Introduce the aim, response options, and right to pass. | Participation choices do not require a reason. |
| 5–15 | Read the case. Separate reported observations, plausible explanations, and unsupported claims. | Notifications, reward-related responses, and sleep conditions are different questions. Feeling tension does not establish neural injury. |
| 15–25 | Read the evidence summaries below and consult the linked notes as needed. | Match each finding to its actual population, setting, and outcome. |
| 25–35 | Choose one environmental change and one optional practice or equivalent alternative. | Address workload, interface, or group expectations as well as individual choices. |
| 35–40 | Allow up to three minutes for the chosen practice or alternative, then a quiet transition. | No group breathing tempo or physical correction. Learners may stop, remain still, or continue case analysis. |
| 40–50 | Draft a small personal or fictional experiment using the prompts below. | Treat useful, neutral, uncomfortable, and not tried as legitimate outcomes. |
| 50–60 | Introduce a constraint, revise the plan, and complete the exit explanation. | Preserve access and necessary contact; assess reasoning rather than relaxation. |

## Three evidence distinctions to teach

- A notification experiment found interference with a particular attention task. It did not establish permanent loss of attention or brain injury. See **E1** in the [evidence notes](https://github.com/TrivianInstitute/embodiment-commons/blob/main/research-and-reading/digital-life-evidence.md).
- A study of adolescents and social endorsement measured behavior and fMRI responses. It did not directly measure dopamine depletion or show that yoga repairs a digital injury. See **E2** in the same notes.
- Research on specific breathing interventions has mixed comparisons and outcomes. It does not validate every breathing pattern or the Commons' original practices as a treatment for screen use. See **E5–E6** in the same notes.

The course's movement and rest units address other questions with their own sources. Do not combine them into a claim that every screen activity has the same effect.

## Build a plan

Use the source course's [workbook](https://github.com/TrivianInstitute/embodiment-commons/blob/main/courses/embodied-digital-life/workbook.md), or answer these prompts on paper:

1. Name a particular situation and a desired change, such as ending a planning session at a chosen time. Do not set a biological repair target.
2. Choose one feasible change: silence a nonessential notification category, display a stopping point, adjust the workspace, or negotiate a shared reply expectation.
3. Choose an optional course practice, an ordinary break, or continued external observation. State how to stop or adapt it.
4. Decide what to notice across a few occasions: whether the stopping point was usable, whether instructions were comfortable, or what barrier remained. Record no private content.
5. Decide when to keep, change, or discontinue the plan. A small personal experiment cannot establish a general causal mechanism.

**Constraint to introduce:** Noor must keep an accessibility service and an urgent family contact channel active. Revise any plan that depended on switching the entire phone off. Then ask what the study group or institution could change so that the burden does not fall entirely on Noor.

## Exit explanation and feedback

A plausible explanation is: “Noor's observations justify examining interruptions, posture, and the transition into rest. They do not establish dopamine damage. Noor could keep essential services active, mute one nonessential notification category, and set an end-of-session note. An optional supported pause or small comfortable movement could be tried, with an ordinary break available. The group could also change expectations about late replies. Noor can report whether the plan was useful without claiming that it healed the brain.”

Look for a bounded claim, an environmental action, meaningful choice, and a revision after the constraint. Do not grade reported calmness, movement, personal disclosure, or reduced screen minutes. Use [reasoning evidence](../assessment/reasoning-evidence.md) and [access guidance](../adaptation/access-and-choice.md). Invite feedback on the resource through [Learning in Practice](https://github.com/TrivianInstitute/learning-in-practice); keep personal observations and learner work out of public issues.
